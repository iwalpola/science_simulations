The one fact this whole sheet rests on

Mr grams of a substance is exactly 1 mole of it.

Mr of water is 18, so 18 g of water is 1 mole of water.
Mr of carbon dioxide is 44, so 44 g of CO2 is 1 mole of CO2.

That single sentence is the bridge between grams (what a balance shows you) and moles (how chemists count particles). Everything else on the sheet is proportion: if 18 g is 1 mole, then 36 g is 2 moles, 9 g is 0.5 moles, 90 g is 5 moles.

It works because of how the mole was defined. One mole is 6.022 × 1023 particles, and that number was chosen precisely so that this comes out tidy. If that is new, the Avogadro constant page covers it.

So step one is always the same: work out Mr. Until you have that number you cannot start. If you are shaky on it, do the relative formula mass page first.

The ratio method

Once you have Mr, write down two rows. The top row is the fact you just worked out. The bottom row is what the question actually gives you, with a ? where the answer goes.

Why cross-multiplying is safe. The two columns are in proportion, so the two diagonal products are always equal. That is true whichever box is empty — which is why the same setup answers "find the moles", "find the mass" and even "find the Mr". You do not have to decide whether to multiply or divide before you start. Set it up, cross-multiply, then rearrange.

The top row always has a 1 in the moles column. That is the whole point of Mr: it is the mass of exactly one mole.

Check it looks sensible

Before you write the answer down, ask which number should be bigger.

If the mass is…then the number of moles is…
bigger than Mr grams (90 g of water, Mr 18)more than 1 mole (5 moles) ✓
smaller than Mr grams (5.8 g of C4H10, Mr 58)less than 1 mole (0.1 moles) ✓
tiny (20.6 mg of gold, Mr 197)a very small fraction of a mole ✓

If you get 5 moles from 5.8 g of something with an Mr of 58, you have divided the wrong way round. This check catches it every time.

A tonne is 1000 kg, and each kg is 1000 g, so a tonne is 1 000 000 g. (The sheet writes "tons"; take it to mean tonnes.) A milligram is a thousandth of a gram, so you divide — a few mg is a small number of grams, and it should look small.

Convert the mass, never the Mr. Mr of NH3 is 17 whether you weighed out milligrams or tonnes of it. Only the number in the bottom-left box changes.

Significant figures, and typing big numbers

Significant figures. Start counting at the first digit that isn't zero. 3.1875 × 106 to 3 s.f. → 3.19 × 106. 0.00010457 to 3 s.f. → 1.05 × 10−4. The power of ten is not a significant figure and never changes when you round.

Keep trailing zeros when they count. 90.0 ÷ 18 = 5 exactly, but the question wants 3 s.f., so write 5.00. Writing 5 throws away the precision you were given.

On the calculator. Use the ×10x button (sometimes EXP or EE) rather than typing x10^, which gets misread.

To type an answer into the boxes on this page, write 3.19e6 or 3.19x10^6, and 1.05e-4 for small ones.

Cheat sheet

  • Mr grams = 1 mole. That is the whole topic.
  • Step 1: work out Mr from the periodic table. Multiply each Ar by how many atoms, add them up.
  • Step 2: convert the mass to grams if it isn't already. kg × 1000, tonnes × 1 000 000, mg ÷ 1000.
  • Step 3: two rows — Mr g : 1 mol on top, the question's numbers underneath with a ?.
  • Step 4: cross-multiply (the two diagonals are equal), then rearrange for the ?.
  • If you'd rather have the formula: moles = mass ÷ Mr and mass = moles × Mr. Both fall straight out of the ratio, so you can always rebuild them.
  • Sanity check: mass bigger than Mr grams → more than 1 mole. Smaller → less than 1 mole.
  • Round to the s.f. asked for, at the end, and give the unit: mol or g. Mr has no unit.
