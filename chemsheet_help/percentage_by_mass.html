Percentage by mass, explained

Help for Chemsheets GCSE 1413. The question is always the same one: out of the whole compound, how much of the mass is that element? Everything you need comes off the periodic table. Work down the page — the answers are hidden until you press a button.

The idea: split the mass into slices

Imagine you could weigh one formula unit of a compound and then separate it into piles, one pile per element. Percentage by mass asks how big one pile is compared with the whole thing.

Here is water. Press the buttons to change which element you are asking about — the sum changes, but the picture doesn't.

Notice that the percentages of every element in a compound add up to 100%. That is a free check on your answers: in water, 11.1% hydrogen + 88.9% oxygen = 100%.

Why hydrogen is such a small slice. Water is two hydrogens and only one oxygen — yet oxygen is nearly nine tenths of the mass. Percentage by mass is not about how many atoms there are; it is about how heavy they are. One oxygen atom (16) outweighs two hydrogens (2) eight times over.

Where every number comes from

There is only one source of data in this whole topic: the bigger number in each periodic table box, the relative atomic mass Ar. The elements used on this sheet are lit up.

Read the bigger number. Copper is Ar = 63.5 (the 29 underneath is the atomic number — protons, not mass). Chlorine is 35.5. Those halves are meant to be there; don't round them.

The method: three lines, every time

1. Work out Mr of the whole compound — multiply each Ar by how many atoms of it there are, and add.

2. Work out the mass of just your element — its Ar × how many of it are in the formula. This is the number people most often get wrong, because they forget to multiply.

3. Divide, then × 100.

% by mass = 100 × mass of your element in the formulaMr of the whole compound

The worked example from the top of the sheet

% by mass of Fe in Fe2O3:

The number 2 has to appear twice. Once when you build Mr (2 × 56 = 112 of iron, plus 3 × 16 = 48 of oxygen, giving 160) and once on the top of the fraction (112, not 56). If the top of your fraction is a bare Ar straight off the periodic table, check whether the formula has more than one of that atom.

A percentage can never be more than 100. If yours is, you have divided the wrong way round — put Mr on the bottom.

Round sensibly, at the end. Three significant figures is right for almost all of these: 88.9%, 43.4%, 9.66%. Don't round Mr part-way through.

Naming help for questions 8, 9 and 10

Three of the questions give you a name instead of a formula. You have to write the formula first, and then nothing is different. The rule is the charges have to cancel out.

NameThe ionsWhy the formula comes out like that
chromium(III) oxideCr3+ and O2− The roman numeral is the charge on the metal: III means 3+. You need 2 Cr (6+) to balance 3 O (6−) → Cr2O3
calcium hydroxideCa2+ and OH− You need two hydroxides to balance one calcium. Two of a group of atoms means brackets → Ca(OH)2
ammonium iodideNH4+ and I− 1+ and 1− already cancel, so one of each and no brackets needed → NH4I

Ammonium (NH4+, positive) and ammonia (NH3, a neutral molecule) are different things. The sheet asks for ammonium.

Cheat sheet

  • % by mass = 100 × (mass of that element ÷ Mr of the compound).
  • Mass of that element = its Ar × how many atoms of it are in the formula. Multiply. Every time.
  • A number outside a bracket multiplies everything inside: Ca(NO3)2 has 6 oxygens, not 3.
  • A dot in the formula (CuSO4·5H2O) means water is part of the crystal. Count it — in both the Mr and the element total.
  • An element on its own (Br2) is 100% that element. Nothing else is in it.
  • All the percentages of a compound add up to 100%. Use it to check.
  • Answers above 100% mean the fraction is upside down. Mr goes on the bottom.
  • Round at the end, to 3 significant figures, and write the % sign.
